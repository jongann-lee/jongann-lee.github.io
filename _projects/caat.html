Coordination of Heterogeneous Teams
with Uncertain Task Requirements

Jongann Lee, Melkior Ornik
University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
arXiv preprint · 2026
Paper Code arXiv

Abstract

Coordinating a team of heterogeneous agents to complete tasks with unknown requirements is a challenging multi-agent planning problem. Agents differ in their ability to discover task requirements and in their ability to engage tasks, so the planner must coordinate task discovery, execution, and staging. This paper introduces a hierarchical planner that uses a graph neural network policy to assign actions to agents, then finds a path to the action's location using a shortest-path algorithm. The central part of our approach is the CAAT graph, which is a graph representation of the planning context consisting of the agents, tasks, capabilities, and actions. We demonstrate that our learned planner can jointly coordinate a heterogeneous team to simultaneously discover task requirements and complete tasks in a shorter time compared to baselines that optimize task discovery and task engagement independently. Furthermore, we show that our policy can zero-shot plan on out-of-distribution settings by training only on an outdoor map then transferring without finetuning to an indoor map with more agents, tasks, and capabilities than seen during training.

Heterogeneous team coordination when there are multiple capabilities

Envision a scenario where satellite imagery has identified threats at known locations across mountainous terrain. A heterogeneous team is tasked with eliminating these threats. Although the target locations are known, the capabilities required to eliminate each target remain uncertain. Some members possess specialized engagement capabilities but have limited vision, like a bomb defuser wearing a bulky mask. Others are lightly equipped scouts; they can identify threats from distant viewpoints by traveling to vantage points (like the top of a hill), but cannot eliminate them. Committing an agent to an incompatible task can result in its loss, hampering the team's ability to complete the objective.

Our solution: CAAT Graph + HGT

Our solution is the following three steps:

  1. Build the CAAT graph, a representation of the planning context.
  2. Compute node embeddings on the CAAT graph using a heterogeneous graph transformer, then define a stochastic policy using the agent and action embeddings.
  3. Train this policy using REINFORCE.

Building the CAAT graph

Encoding the capabilities and actions as separate nodes allows a pretrained GNN to handle any number of capabilities and provides a consistent set of actions.

Learning to coordinate scouting and execution

A heterogeneous graph transformer processes the different node and edge types. An MLP scores agent–action pairs, and a sequential decoder assigns feasible actions. A shortest-path planner then routes agents to the assigned locations.

We pretrain the policy using REINFORCE on 12,800 simulated rollouts on WV. During evaluation, all policy weights remain frozen: transfer to a new map requires no fine-tuning.

CAAT Graph + HGT can perform out of distribution planning

We first evaluate on the training map WV, to evaluate whether our policy learns to plan effectively within the training domain. We then evaluate on unseen maps WA and KTH, which introduce progressively larger deviations from the training setting. All policy weights remain frozen during evaluation.

  • WV: The training map, with 3–6 agents, 5–9 tasks, and 3 non-scout capabilities.
  • WA: A different outdoor map with a different starting position, but the same terrain traversal rule, viewshed visibility, and ranges of capability, agent, and task counts.
  • KTH: The largest deviations, with a different traversal model, different visibility, and more capabilities, agents, and tasks than seen during training: 8 agents, 14 tasks, and 5 non-scout capabilities. This indoor map has 4.6 times as many nodes as WV or WA.

Scout Then Engage finishes scouting before executing tasks. Engage While Scout runs scouting and execution simultaneously, optimizing the two subproblems separately. Our policy coordinates them jointly and can stage agents before requirements are revealed.

On all three maps, our pretrained CAAT Graph + HGT has lower average regret than both baselines. On WV and WA, our method results in 17.3% and 20.3% lower average regret, respectively, compared to the best baseline. Even on KTH, an unseen indoor map with more agents, tasks, and capabilities than seen during training, our method achieves 12.4% lower average regret than the best baseline. These results show that our method zero-shot transfers to out-of-distribution settings without any fine-tuning.

Our method is better than both baselines for more than 77% of the test problems on each map: 143 of 180 on WV, 159 of 180 on WA, and 7 of 9 on KTH. It improves across a wide range of problems, both within and beyond the training domain.
